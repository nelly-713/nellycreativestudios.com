Buying Guide

Is Gold Jewelry
a Good Investment?

July 2026  ·  By Nelly Mendoza-Mendoza

Gold has been trading above $4,000 an ounce for most of 2026 — up roughly 24% over the past year alone, and more than double what it was trading at five years ago. People are noticing. I get some version of this question often, usually from someone standing at the counter deciding between two pieces: "Is this actually worth something, or am I just buying something pretty?"

The honest answer is both — but they're not the same thing, and the difference matters if you're thinking about jewelry as a store of value and not just an accessory.

The Metal Itself Does Hold Value

Gold's track record as a store of value is real and well documented. It has appreciated significantly over the past several decades, and it tends to hold up — sometimes even gain — during exactly the kind of economic uncertainty that erodes other assets. That's why central banks hold it, and why "safe haven" is the phrase you'll hear attached to it again and again.

So in that narrow sense, yes: the gold in a piece of fine jewelry has real, calculable value, tied directly to the current market price and the purity of the metal. A 22 karat piece contains roughly 91.6% pure gold by weight; an 18 karat piece contains 75%; 14 karat contains about 58.5%. That gold content doesn't disappear. It's there in the piece, and it will always be worth something, even melted down to nothing but raw metal.

But Jewelry Isn't the Same as Buying Gold

Here's the part most people don't think about: when you buy a piece of gold jewelry, you're not paying the spot price for the metal. You're paying for the metal plus the craftsmanship, the design, the labor, and — depending on where you buy — the markup. That premium is the cost of turning raw material into something wearable, and it's real value in its own right. But it's not liquid the way bullion or a gold ETF is. If you tried to resell a piece of jewelry the week after buying it, you would very likely get back less than you paid, because you'd typically be offered something closer to melt value — not the full retail price that included craftsmanship.

This is the honest distinction: gold bars and gold coins are an investment vehicle. Gold jewelry is a wearable object that happens to be made of a material with real, independent value. Those are related but different things, and conflating them is where people get the wrong idea.

So What Actually Holds Value in Fine Jewelry?

If you're choosing jewelry with an eye toward it holding its value well — not as a substitute for a diversified portfolio, but as something that won't lose meaning or worth over decades — a few things matter more than others:

Karat purity. Higher karat gold contains more actual gold by weight, which means more of what you paid for is tied directly to the metal's market value rather than to a specific brand or trend. We work almost exclusively in 22 karat gold for this reason — it's a deeper, warmer color than the 14k and 18k you'll find at most jewelers, and a meaningfully higher percentage of every piece is gold itself.

Quality of craftsmanship. A well-made piece — solid construction, secure settings, techniques built to last generations — holds up physically over decades in a way that mass-produced, hollow, or poorly soldered pieces simply don't. Jewelry that needs constant repair isn't holding its value; it's losing it a little at a time.

Timeless design over trend-driven design. A piece built around this season's shape will feel dated in a few years. A piece built around a technique or a form that's lasted for centuries tends to still feel relevant decades from now. This is part of why I work so much in ancient Greek gold granulation — it's a technique that's been valued for over 2,700 years, not two seasons.

Natural, well-documented gemstones. Rarity and provenance matter for colored stones the same way purity matters for gold. A well-documented natural gemstone tends to hold its value far better than an unidentified or treated stone of similar appearance.

Keep your documentation. A receipt, an appraisal, and — for anything custom — notes on the materials used. If you ever do want to insure a piece, sell it, or pass it down, this is what makes its value provable rather than just asserted.

The Honest Bottom Line

I wouldn't tell anyone to buy fine jewelry instead of a retirement account. That's not what it's for, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you something. But I also don't think "it's not a pure investment" means it's not worth something real. A well-made, high-karat piece is a genuinely different asset than a piece of costume jewelry or a fast-fashion accessory — it carries real material value that doesn't evaporate, on top of the part that actually matters most: you get to wear it, live with it, and eventually hand it to someone else.

That's the version of "investment" I actually believe in — something built to outlast a trend cycle, made well enough to be worth keeping, and meaningful enough to be worth giving away.
